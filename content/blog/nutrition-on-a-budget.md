Good nutrition is not about expensive imported superfoods. Nairobi's own markets (Marikiti, Gikomba, Toi, your local mama mboga) stock everything a family needs to eat well. As a doctor, the question I hear most is: *"How do I feed my family healthy food on a tight budget?"* Here is the advice I give my patients every week.

## Build every plate around the traditional trio

The Kenyan meal pattern of a starch, a vegetable relish and a protein is genuinely sound. Your job is simply to improve the quality of each part:

- **Starch:** Mix maize flour with millet or sorghum flour for ugali with more fibre and iron. Wholegrain options keep you full longer and steady your blood sugar.
- **Vegetables:** Sukuma wiki, managu, terere, kunde and cabbage are nutrient powerhouses that cost a few shillings a bunch. Steam lightly or add them last so they keep their vitamins.
- **Protein:** Ndengu (green grams), beans, njahi and omena give you excellent protein for far less than meat. Aim for at least three legume dinners a week.

## Shop smart, waste nothing

Buy fruit and vegetables that are **in season**; mangoes, oranges and bananas rotate through the year at very low prices. Visit the market late in the day for discounts, buy dry goods in bulk with neighbours, and freeze sukuma or managu (blanched for two minutes first) when you find a good deal.

## Small changes with a big health payoff

- Swap deep-frying for dry-roasting or stewing, especially for omena, which is rich in calcium.
- Cut sugary drinks entirely; water, unsweetened chai or homemade mursik hydrate just as well and protect children's teeth.
- Watch salt: hypertension is rising fast in Nairobi. Season with garlic, ginger, dhania and pilipili instead of extra salt.
- For children, a boiled egg and a banana is a far better afternoon snack than crisps.

> If you remember one thing: half the plate vegetables, a quarter protein, a quarter starch. That works at kibanda prices and at supermarket prices.
